The tide clock

MBBy Maren Brightwater·14 October 2026·9 min read

Every harbour town keeps its own time. In Fernway it is set by the tide clock on the old customs house, a brass dial with a single hand that sweeps from low to high twice a day. Fishermen glance at it the way office workers check their phones.

The clock was installed in 1884 by a ferry company that has long since disappeared. Its mechanism is simple: a float in a stilling well, a chain, a counterweight, and a set of gears that reduce the rise and fall of the water to a quarter turn of the hand.

Reading the water

A tide table tells you when; the water itself tells you how. Wind from the west piles water against the breakwater and can lift high tide by half a metre. A long calm spell does the opposite. Locals learn to read both the dial and the colour of the channel before they leave the slip.

Low water exposes the mudflats east of the harbour, a grey-green plain crossed by narrow creeks. Wading birds arrive within minutes: oystercatchers first, then curlews, then the small, nervous sandpipers that run in step with the retreating waterline.

A walk at low tide

The marked path to Holm Island is safe for about three hours either side of low water. Posts every fifty metres show the way. The rule that every guide repeats is the same: start back when the second post behind you begins to stand in water, not later.

Safety — Check the tide table on the day. Allow 75 minutes each way. Do not cross in fog.

Halfway across there is a refuge hut on stilts with a ladder and a bell. It has been used eleven times in the last decade, mostly by walkers who misread the tide table by an hour after the clocks changed.

Keeping records

Since 1951 the harbour master has written the observed high water mark in a ledger next to the predicted value. The ledgers fill a shelf in the customs house. Read in sequence, they show the slow rise of the sea more clearly than any chart: the average difference has grown by eleven centimetres.

Volunteers are now copying the ledgers into a spreadsheet, page by page. The handwriting changes every few years as harbour masters come and go; one wrote in green ink, another added a small drawing of the weather each day.

What changes next

A new flood gate is planned for 2028. It will close the inner harbour during storm surges and, for the first time, separate the town from the tide for a few hours at a time. The tide clock will keep turning on the outside wall, measuring water that no longer reaches the quay.

Until then, the clock remains the town's most accurate instrument and its most consulted one. Walk past it at any hour and someone will be looking up, doing the small calculation that decides whether to go out now or wait for the turn.
